## POST 1

Date:May 2026
Post:A finding from our AEO experiment has changed the way we think about prompt selection.

When we ran our target prompts through Clearscope's Prompt Tracking, we noticed something in the fan-out data that wasn't obvious at first: the frequency percentage next to each AI web search isn't just telling you how popular that search is. It's telling you how stable it is.

Here's what we learned.

Prompts with fan-out queries above 30% frequency are strong signals. That means Gemini is consistently reaching for that search to construct its answer… week after week, run after run. Those are the queries worth building content against, because they're likely to stay relevant.

Anything below 15%? The LLMs are essentially spitballing. The responses are so variable that the same prompt might produce completely different web searches next time you run it. There's so much fluctuation that even if you rank for those queries today, the AI may not be pulling them tomorrow.

The practical implication: don't chase low-frequency fan-out queries. They're a sign that the AI hasn't settled on a consistent way to answer that prompt yet. Wait until the LLMs simmer down and focus your content investment on the queries the AI is reaching for reliably.

This is one of those things you can only learn by running the experiment yourself.

More findings coming as we go.

## POST 2

Date:May 2026
Post:For those of you following along, we started an AEO experiment using Clearscope's own platform to test whether a repeatable content process can earn brand mentions in AI-generated responses. Our target prompt: "Answer Engine Optimization."

This is our first check-in, about 3 weeks later.

Here's what's happening.

On our strongest prompt — "tools for tracking AI citations and brand mentions in LLMs" — Clearscope's brand mention rate has climbed to over 38% in just 3 weeks, up from 0% when we started. That movement came directly from publishing content that targets the exact queries Gemini uses to construct its answer.

That's the methodology working in real time. 

So next we asked ourselves: why haven't the other two prompts moved?

This is where it gets really interesting. On both remaining prompts, it's not just Clearscope that isn't showing up. Even the biggest, most established players in the space are barely registering. Semrush and Ahrefs, who dominate nearly every tool-related query we track, are getting single-digit or low double-digit visibility on these prompts.

That's the real signal. When no brand is getting meaningful traction, it means the prompt intent is wrong. Gemini isn't interpreting these as "recommend a tool" questions. It's treating them as educational queries and returning tactical content instead of brand recommendations. That's not a content problem you can write your way out of. It's a prompt selection problem. And catching it early is one of the most valuable things Prompt Tracking does. 

So naturally the next question is: where do we go from here?

We will continue to track the two prompts that haven't moved. And at the same time we've identified a replacement prompt. It's one where Clearscope is already showing up at 25%, but competitors are dominating: Semrush at 98%, Surfer at 85%, Ahrefs at 85%.

That's actually the ideal starting point. We already have a foothold. The question is whether targeted content can close the gap. We're going to find out.

Update coming in a few weeks.

Stay tuned.

## POST 3

Date:March 2026
Post:Everyone keeps saying “AI is the new search.” 

But here’s the part people skip: If AI is search, then every model is a different search engine and they all behave very differently.

* Gemini feels like Google: Constantly searching the web. Recalculating. Expanding queries.
* GPT feels more like early Yahoo. Structured. Curated. Harder to disrupt quickly.
* Perplexity is basically Bing with excellent citations.
* Grok behaves more like Twitter search than a traditional index.
* Claude feels like Ask Jeeves if Jeeves went to grad school.

The comparison is a little funny. But the implication is serious: When people say “optimize for AI,” they’re usually treating five completely different retrieval systems like one platform.

And visibility works differently in each of them.
